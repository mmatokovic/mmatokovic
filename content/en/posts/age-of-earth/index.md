The story of Earth's age is a fascinating journey through time, blending mythology, scientific inquiry and revolutionary ideas. It begins with **James Ussher**, a 17th-century Irish archbishop (1581–1656). Working from the Bible as his main source, he calculated the date of creation as **4004 BC**, and published it in his _Annales_ in the 1650s.[^1] At the time, mythological and religious texts were the authoritative guides to the world's history. The same was true in the Islamic world, where the Quran served as the primary source for human origins. Ussher's conclusion was widely accepted.

> The Creation of the Universe was said, by Ussher, to have taken place on: "the entrance of the night preceding the 23rd day of October... the year before Christ 4004"
{caption="— James Ussher"}

It is worth remembering that this date is more than a fun fact. It shows how completely Europeans once relied on scripture for their sense of where humanity came from. Much of what follows is the story of that reliance giving way to evidence. It is also the background you need if you want to understand why [rock art](/posts/south-african-rock-art) and the minds that made it matter so much to the study of religion.

## The Shift from Mythology to Science

The scientific revolution slowly loosened the hold of scripture on natural history. **Newton** explained the motions of the planets, and **Galileo** showed through his telescope that the Earth was not the centre of the universe. Some thinkers began to turn the same kind of inquiry on the ground beneath their feet.

Three competing pictures emerged. **Catastrophism** held that the Earth's features came from sudden, violent events, above all **Noah's flood**. The strata in rock were read as sediment that settled out of the flood waters, and canyons as channels cut by the rushing water. **Gradualism** and **uniformitarianism** held that small, slow processes, still at work today, add up over long periods to produce great change.

The key figure here is **James Hutton**, a Scottish geologist of the late 18th century. His _Theory of the Earth; or an Investigation of the Laws Observable in the Composition, Dissolution, and Restoration of Land upon the Globe_ appeared in the _Transactions of the Royal Society of Edinburgh_ in 1788.[^2] It argued that the Earth was far older than the Bible literalists allowed. Hutton saw in the rocks a long cycle of erosion, deposition and uplift, with, as he famously put it, "no vestige of a beginning, no prospect of an end". According to Hutton, the present is the key to understanding the past. That notion undermined the prevailing biblical timeline and laid the foundation for modern geology.

Not all early geologists agreed. Catastrophism, with its flood geology, stayed popular for some time. It was **Charles Lyell**, an English geologist, who turned Hutton's ideas into a professional science. His _Principles of Geology_ (1830–1833)[^3] carried a long subtitle that summed up the whole programme: _Being an Attempt to Explain the Former Changes of the Earth's Surface, by Reference to Causes Now in Operation_. He studied volcanic Mount Etna, where he saw how lava flows had built up the mountain layer by layer.

## The Temple of Serapis

The frontispiece of Lyell's book shows three columns standing in water. They belong to the so-called **Temple of Serapis** (really a Roman market building) at **Pozzuoli**, on the Bay of Naples. The columns are bored by marine clams, which leave a band of holes some distance up the shafts. The temple must therefore have been built on dry land, then sunk below the sea long enough for the molluscs to do their work, then raised again. Lyell used this as proof that the ground itself rises and falls, gradually and still today. It was a decisive blow against the idea of a fixed, unchanging Earth.

Lyell also watched sediment accumulating in the present day and then considered how much of it the world's rock successions contain. A crude estimate puts the Earth's age in the millions of years. This is the time that **Darwin**, who read the _Principles_ on his voyage, needed for evolution to work.

## Charles Darwin and Evolution

In the 19th century, the work of **Charles Darwin** added another layer to our understanding of Earth's history. Darwin's seminal book, _On the Origin of Species_ (1859)[^4], introduced the theory of evolution through natural selection. On the voyage of **HMS Beagle**, which circled the globe, he noticed that related species differ in ways that suit their surroundings. The Galápagos finches have beaks fitted to their diets. The giant tortoises differ in shell shape from island to island: the saddle-backed forms live where food hangs high on dry islands, and the domed forms live where grass is plentiful.

Darwin also looked closely at domestication. Pigeons, dogs, cattle and crops had changed enormously under human breeding. If artificial selection could make a pug from a wolf-like ancestor, he asked, what could nature do given enough time? A long Earth was the missing ingredient, and Lyell had supplied it. The popular phrase "survival of the fittest" is often misunderstood. It was coined by the philosopher **Herbert Spencer** and adopted by Darwin in later editions. It does not mean the strongest or fastest, only the best suited to a given environment.

Darwin's ideas were controversial, particularly in relation to human evolution. Even Darwin struggled with some of them; in an 1860 letter to the botanist **Asa Gray** he wrote, _"The sight of a feather in a peacock's tail, whenever I gaze at it, makes me sick!"_[^5] The tail troubled him because natural selection alone could not explain it. His answer, in _The Descent of Man_ (1871), was **sexual selection**: traits like the peacock's extravagant tail develop because they attract mates, even if they do not directly aid survival.

## The Oxford Debate

The tension came to a head in June 1860 at a meeting of the British Association in Oxford. Bishop **Samuel Wilberforce** is said to have asked **Thomas Huxley** whether it was through his grandfather or his grandmother that he claimed descent from an ape. Huxley is reported to have replied that he would rather have an ape for an ancestor than a man who used his gifts to bring ridicule into serious scientific discussion. No verbatim record survives, and the accounts differ, so the exact words are uncertain.[^6] **Robert FitzRoy**, the captain of the Beagle, a deeply religious man, is also recalled as protesting, holding up a Bible. He took his own life five years later, in 1865.

## The Discovery of Ancient Evidence

Evidence for deep human antiquity was accumulating at the same time. In 1856 workers found a skeleton in the Neander Valley in Germany, which **Johann Carl Fuhlrott** recognised as a distinct, robust and archaic kind of human, with a heavy brow ridge and a large nose. This was the **Neanderthal**, and it shaped the popular image of the "caveman". In 1899 further **Neanderthal fossils** came to light at **Hušnjakovo Brdo** near Krapina, Croatia, where **Dragutin Gorjanović-Kramberger** excavated them.[^7]

Around 1860 the French palaeontologist **Édouard Lartet** found bones of extinct Ice Age animals engraved by human hands. That showed people had lived alongside mammoths and woolly rhinoceroses. Then, in 1879, **Marcelino Sanz de Sautuola** noticed the painted bison on the ceiling of **Altamira Cave** in Spain. His daughter María is said to have been the first to spot them.[^8] His discovery was met with skepticism, and many experts accused him of forgery. Only after his death was he vindicated, as similar caves were found in France. These [rock art](/posts/south-african-rock-art) paintings are among the earliest known examples of human creativity, and they show the depth of our history.

![Chauvet Cave](apr2015_h07_chauvetcave.webp "Chauvet Cave")

This was also the era of colonial expansion. Europeans met living hunter-gatherer peoples, such as the San of the Kalahari, Native Americans and Aboriginal Australians. Many treated them as survivors of humanity's **primordial state**, a view that was often condescending and wrong, but that shaped how prehistory was imagined. The San are now better known for the richness of their spiritual life and their [trance-based rock art](/posts/between-worlds), and for the way such art has been read as a window on early [religion and ritual](/posts/south-african-rock-art).

## The Development of Scientific Dating Methods

Radioactivity finally made it possible to measure deep time. In 1907 **Bertram Boltwood** showed that uranium decays to lead and used the ratio to date rocks, and **Arthur Holmes** developed the method in the following years. **Uranium-238** decays to **lead-206** with a half-life of about 4.47 billion years, and **uranium-235** decays to **lead-207** with a half-life of about 704 million years. Measuring the ratio of parent to daughter atoms in a mineral gives its age. Zircon crystals are ideal because they take up uranium when they form but exclude lead.

In 2001, zircons from the **Jack Hills** of Western Australia were dated at up to about 4.4 billion years, the oldest known fragments of Earth's crust.[^9] The age of the Earth itself, about 4.54 billion years, was established by **Clair Patterson** in 1956 from lead isotopes in meteorites. These measurements confirmed what Hutton and Lyell had argued on the evidence of rocks, that the planet was inconceivably older than the biblical timeline allowed.

The fossil record also began to reveal our ancestors. **Lucy**, an **Australopithecus afarensis** found in Ethiopia in 1974 by **Donald Johanson** and his team, lived around 3.2 million years ago.[^10] Her skull and brain were ape-like, but her pelvis and legs show she walked upright. So walking on two legs came before large brains, which overturned what many had expected. Since then many more species have been found, including _Homo habilis_, an early toolmaker, and _Homo erectus_, associated with early evidence of fire, along with Neanderthals and our own species, _Homo sapiens_.

## Conclusion

The study of Earth's age has dramatically changed our understanding of the planet and our place on it. From Ussher's biblical chronology to the scientific breakthroughs of Hutton, Lyell, Darwin and others, geology has grown into a rigorous science. The evidence for an ancient Earth is now overwhelming, and each new discovery, whether fossils, ancient cave paintings or advanced dating methods, further deepens our understanding of Earth's long and complex history.

Science has not eliminated the need for origin stories, though. Where medieval Europe had Genesis, today's imagination often turns to films about aliens seeding life. Understanding religion and mythology means taking both the physical evidence of our origins and the stories people tell about them seriously.
